# Chapter 8 — Solutions: Embeddings

Identifiers match the exercises in `book/chapters/08-embeddings.md`. Numbers are illustrative unless they come from running `book/projects/examples/ch08/demo.py`.

## Knowledge questions

**K1.** For unit vectors `||a|| = ||b|| = 1`, so `cos(a, b) = a·b / (||a|| ||b||) = a·b`: cosine and dot product are the same number. Squared Euclidean distance expands to `||a||² + ||b||² - 2 a·b = 2 - 2 cos(a, b)`, a strictly decreasing function of cosine, so sorting by ascending distance gives the same order as sorting by descending cosine. All three rankings coincide. For unnormalized vectors they diverge whenever lengths differ: with query `[0.8, 0.2, 0]` and documents `[0.9, 0.1, 0]` and `[6, 6, 2]`, cosine prefers the first (closer direction, about 0.99 versus 0.83) while dot product prefers the second (6.0 versus 0.74) purely because it is long. If length is meaningless for the model, the dot product rewarded noise.

**K2.** Matryoshka training applies the contrastive loss to several prefixes of the output vector (for example the first 64, 128, 256 dimensions and the full vector) in addition to the full vector. The model is therefore pushed to put the most discriminative information in the leading coordinates, and a prefix behaves like a smaller embedding of the same space. A prefix of a unit vector has norm below 1 (it is the square root of the energy in the kept coordinates), and that norm varies per vector. Without re-normalization, dot-product scores are scaled by a per-document factor, so documents whose energy happens to sit in later coordinates are penalized and the ranking changes. Re-normalizing restores cosine semantics on the prefix.

**K3.** The index would contain vectors from two unrelated coordinate systems. Similarities between a new-model query and old-model documents are meaningless numbers: old documents will be ranked essentially at random relative to new ones, often systematically above or below them depending on how the two similarity distributions differ. Queries also have to be embedded with one model, so half the corpus is always being compared in the wrong space. Nothing errors; quality just drops. The correct migration: define the new embedding space, build a second index from the source text for every item (not only new ones), double-write new documents to both indexes during the build, evaluate the new index in shadow on the gold queries (recall@k, MRR, latency), switch reads when it passes, keep the old index for a rollback window, then retire it. `plan_reembed` gives the token and cost estimate up front.

**K4.** Contrastive training only learns to separate what positives and negatives separate. The positives say "these must be close," the negatives say "these must be apart," and any distinction that never appears between a positive and its negatives is free to collapse. If negatives are random passages, the model learns topical separation and nothing finer. A general-purpose model trained on web question-answer pairs is likely to miss distinctions such as "SKU 4471 versus SKU 4417," "the policy applies to contractors" versus "the policy does not apply to contractors," or "PTO carryover in the 2025 version" versus "the 2026 version," because those rarely appear as hard negatives in general training data.

**K5.** Anisotropy means the vectors occupy a narrow cone rather than spreading across the sphere, so even unrelated texts have high positive cosine similarity. Detect it by computing the distribution of cosine similarities between random pairs from your corpus (`similarity_profile`): a mean of 0.6 or a p05 well above zero indicates anisotropy. Thresholds do not transfer because the usable range is model-specific: in one model unrelated pairs score 0.05 and duplicates 0.9; in another unrelated pairs score 0.7 and duplicates 0.92. A threshold of 0.8 means "clearly related" in the first and "essentially unrelated" in the second.

**K6.** Because the same model name and the same text can produce different vectors when anything else about the call changes. A key of (model, text) returns stale vectors after a prefix change, a dimension change, a text-preparation fix, or a post-processing change. The space fingerprint in this chapter covers: model, dimensions, text-preparation version, query prefix, passage prefix, the normalization flag, and the post-processing descriptor (for example mean-centering or truncation). The key is then (fingerprint, model, prepared text), implemented by `NamespacedStore` around `CachedEmbeddings`.

## Engineering questions

**E1.** Build a labeled set of at least 200 ticket pairs sampled from the population the job will see: candidate pairs above a low similarity (for example the top-20 neighbors of 100 random tickets), so the set is dominated by hard cases rather than trivially different pairs. Label each as duplicate or not with a written definition ("same requester need, same affected system, same time window"), double-labeling a subset to measure agreement. Include hard negatives deliberately: same store different terminal, same error different day, same category different issue. Because auto-closing hides a real problem from a customer, the precision floor on the labeled set is 1.0 (or at least 0.99 with a confidence interval computed on the positives). Use `sweep_thresholds` and `select_threshold`; if it returns `None`, the model cannot support auto-closing and the feature ships as "suggest link" instead. Pairs between the auto-close threshold and a lower suggestion threshold go to agents as "possible duplicate." Re-validation after any space change is part of the migration checklist: re-embed the labeled pairs with the new space, re-run the sweep, require the floor again, and compare the chosen threshold with the old one (a large shift is expected and is exactly why the old value cannot be reused). Monitor in production: sample auto-closed pairs weekly for human review and alert on any false merge.

**E2.** Build a gold set of 150 or more queries from logistics traffic with labeled relevant documents, including slices for identifiers (tracking IDs, error codes), paraphrases, and every language used by dispatchers. Shortlist hosted models that can be served from an EU region under the data-processing agreement, plus two or three self-hostable models that can run on EU infrastructure. Run the identical evaluation for each: recall@5 and MRR overall and per slice, p50 and p95 query-embedding latency from the application's network location, embedding throughput for a full re-index, and vector size. Hosted costs: per-token fees, rate limits that bound re-index time, dependency on the provider's model lifecycle (forced migrations, silent updates), and data-processing review. Self-hosted costs: GPU or CPU capacity sized for peak query load plus re-index batches, deployment, monitoring and patching, model upgrades handled by the team, but fixed cost, pinned versions, and data never leaving the region. Decide on the slice results that matter for logistics (identifier queries usually), not on the overall average, and consider fine-tuning the self-hosted candidate on logistics query-passage pairs if it is close.

**E3.** Embed the message once per request and pass the vector to the router, the classifier, and retrieval: this removes two of three embedding calls, saving roughly 2 × 180 ms at p95 if they were sequential, or reducing tail risk and cost if they were parallel. Cache query embeddings keyed by space fingerprint and prepared text; repeated FAQ-style questions then cost a cache lookup (single-digit milliseconds). Start the embedding call as soon as the message arrives, in parallel with authentication and conversation loading. If 180 ms remains too high, evaluate a small self-hosted model co-located with the API, which can embed a short query in a few milliseconds (illustrative); the gold-set recall must hold. Expected effect: query-embedding contribution to time-to-first-token falls from up to about 540 ms to about 180 ms on misses and near zero on cache hits.

**E4.** Job design: enumerate chunk IDs with content hashes from the relational store; process them in batches bounded by count and tokens with bounded concurrency matched to the provider's tokens-per-minute limit, with backoff on rate-limit errors; write each batch's vectors to the new index version in a transaction together with a checkpoint row (last chunk ID processed, counts, tokens). On restart, resume from the checkpoint; items are keyed by (chunk ID, index version) so a replayed batch is an idempotent upsert. During the build, the ingestion pipeline double-writes new and changed chunks to both index versions, and deletions are applied to both. When the backfill completes, verify counts match between versions, run the gold-set evaluation and latency benchmark against the new index in shadow (and optionally mirror a sample of live queries to compare result overlap). Cutover criteria: recall@k and MRR at or above the old index within an agreed tolerance on every slice, p95 latency within budget, zero space-mismatch errors. Cutover is a configuration flip of the active index version, which also switches the query-embedding space; both change together. Rollback is flipping back, which works because the old index stays active for writes until retirement. Retire the old version after a grace period.

## Practical exercises

**P1.** Expected implementation: call `truncation_report` with a descending list of dimensions (for example full, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16) and pick the smallest whose recall@3 is within 0.03 of full; with the bag-of-words fake on the chapter's data, half the dimensions keeps recall at 0.906 and a quarter drops to 0.844, so the answer is half. Extend `VectorIndex` (or add a `TwoStageIndex`) that stores both the full matrix and a truncated, re-normalized matrix, records `post_process="truncate:<d>"` in a separate space for the truncated stage, retrieves top-50 by the truncated matrix, rescores those 50 with the full vectors, and returns top-k. Acceptance criteria: a test showing that two-stage results equal full-dimension results on the tiny vocabulary corpus when 50 exceeds the corpus size; recall@3 of two-stage within 0.01 of full on the Northwind queries; memory for the first stage reported.

**P2.** Expected implementation: add 15 lines to a new fixture (for example `identifier_queries.jsonl`) such as "scanner error SH-305", "RET-002 returns API", "webhook 7731", with the documents that contain those identifiers as relevant. Report recall@3 for that slice and for the original set. Note that the fake tokenizer splits "SH-305" into "sh" and "305", and the vocabulary drops digits, so the fake largely loses identifiers, which mirrors a real model's weakness. Implement a lexical boost: extract identifier-like tokens with a regex (letters plus digits, or all-caps codes), compute exact overlap with each candidate's text, and add `boost × overlap` to the cosine score before ranking. Acceptance: identifier-slice recall improves substantially, the original set's recall does not drop by more than 0.02, and a test pins both. The solution should mention that Chapter 12's BM25 hybrid is the production version of this idea.

**P3.** Expected implementation: `MultiCentroidClassifier(per_class=2, ...)` runs `KMeans` within each class that has at least `per_class` examples (fewer examples fall back to one centroid), stores all sub-centroids with their class label, and predicts the class of the most similar sub-centroid, with margin computed between the best sub-centroid and the best sub-centroid of a *different* class. Compare with `CentroidClassifier` and `KNNClassifier(k=5)` using `leave_one_out` under the same abstention rule (for example `min_similarity=0.15, min_margin=0.03`). Report accuracy on answered and coverage for all three. Acceptance: a unit test with a synthetic class made of two distinct sub-topics, where the single centroid misclassifies an item and the multi-centroid classifier gets it right; the leave-one-out table in the README. Expect small or noisy differences on 60 tickets; the solution should say that conclusions require a larger labeled set.

**P4.** Expected implementation: a thin `RedisStore(MutableMapping[str, bytes])` wrapper over a Redis client (`get`, `set` with `ex=ttl_s`, `delete`, `scan_iter` for iteration), passed as `store=` to `EmbeddingPipeline`; `NamespacedStore` already prefixes keys with the fingerprint. Add a metric function counting keys per namespace with `SCAN MATCH emb:<fingerprint>:*` (never `KEYS` in production). Tests use a fake Redis (for example a dict-backed stub implementing the same methods, or `fakeredis` if installed) so they run offline: embed a set of texts with space A, then with space B (different prefix), assert B's first pass has zero cache hits and that A's entries are untouched; assert the TTL is set on written keys. Acceptance criteria also include an integration test marked `integration` against a real `REDIS_URL`.

## Debugging exercises

**D1.** Root cause: the deploy changed something in the query embedding space other than the model name, most likely the query prefix (removed or changed), the text preparation, or a dimension setting, and the query cache is keyed by model and text only, so it kept serving old-space vectors for repeated queries while new queries got new-space vectors, and in addition the new-space query vectors are compared with exemplars embedded in the old space. Similarities rose across the board (a prefix removal on an asymmetric model often inflates scores), so fewer messages fell below the threshold, and the margin test stopped catching ambiguous ones. Confirming telemetry: the space fingerprint on query spans versus the fingerprint stored with the router exemplars (they differ, or would if the fingerprint were recorded); the distribution of best-route scores before and after the deploy (shifted upward); a 94 percent cache hit rate immediately after a space change, which should have dropped to near zero. Fix: namespace the cache by space fingerprint, make the router refuse exemplars from a different space, re-embed exemplars, and re-calibrate the threshold.

**D2.** Lowercasing changes the input text, so the query vectors moved, while document vectors in the unchanged index were produced from text with original casing. The query service is now in a different embedding space from the index (new text-preparation behavior), which the new fingerprint shows. Case often carries signal for identifiers and product names (for example "POS" versus "pos", error codes), so recall dropped. Fix: roll back the normalization change on the query path, or, if lowercasing is desired, bump `TEXT_PREP_VERSION`, treat it as a new space, re-embed the corpus into a new index, evaluate, and cut over both paths together. Prevention: the index should refuse queries whose fingerprint differs from its own (`SpaceMismatchError`), so the deploy fails at the first request instead of degrading silently; and the gold-set recall check should run in CI on any change to text preparation.

**D3.** The provider's update changed the model's similarity distribution: random pairs now average 0.58 instead of 0.21, so the whole scale shifted upward and the old 0.82 threshold now sits much closer to the "related but different" range. Tickets about payment terminals at different stores share most of their wording and now exceed 0.82. Also, if the index holds vectors from before the update and new tickets are embedded after it, the comparison is across spaces. Remediation: stop auto-merging immediately (fall back to suggestions), re-embed all open tickets with the current model, re-run threshold selection on the labeled dedup pairs with the precision floor, review merges made since the update and split wrong ones. Monitoring that would have caught it: a scheduled job embedding a fixed probe set and comparing its vectors and similarity profile with stored baselines (any change in the vectors for identical text means the model changed), an alert on the random-pair mean cosine, and a weekly human sample of auto-merged pairs.

**D4.** Root cause: the mean-centering change made the corpus mean part of the space fingerprint (`post_process = mean-center:<hash of corpus mean>`) and recomputes that mean on every nightly run. Any edit to any document moves the mean slightly (the spans show `tokens_sent_total` drifting from 58,210 to 58,204, so the corpus did change a little), so every night produces a new fingerprint, `index.swap` reports `changed: post_process`, and the space-namespaced cache has zero hits: every section is re-embedded although the model output for unchanged text is identical. The design error is caching after post-processing. The raw model vector depends only on (model, dimensions, prefix, text preparation, text); mean-centering is a cheap linear step that can be applied to cached raw vectors at index and query time. The morning `SpaceMismatchError` happens because the query service loaded the previous night's mean and fingerprint at startup and keeps serving them after the indexer swaps in a new space; the index correctly refuses the stale queries until the query service reloads. Telemetry that reveals it: `cache_hits = 0` with a new `space` value every night, `index.swap` events with `reason: changed: post_process` daily, tokens sent per day jumping from thousands to the full corpus on the release date, and mismatch errors clustered just after each swap. Fix: cache raw vectors under the raw space (no post-processing in the cache namespace); freeze the mean as a versioned artifact recomputed deliberately (for example quarterly, or when drift crosses a threshold), shipped to the indexer and the query service together as one space version; apply centering at write and query time from that artifact. Add an alert on "full re-embed when fewer than N percent of documents changed" and make the query service watch the active index version rather than reading it once at startup.
