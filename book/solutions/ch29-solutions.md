# Chapter 29 — Solutions

Identifiers match the Exercises section of `book/chapters/29-reliability-and-scalability.md`. Numbers are illustrative.

## Knowledge questions

**K1.** Each indicator catches a failure the others miss. Availability catches errors and timeouts but is blind to slowness. Time to first token catches a slow start, which is what users feel in a streaming UI. Completion latency catches long generations and slow tool or retrieval stages after the first token. Task success catches answers that arrive quickly and correctly formatted but wrong or ungrounded. Degraded share catches the case where availability is being bought with quality. If degraded answers count as available and degraded share is not measured, a team can run on the small model or with retrieval disabled for days and report a perfect availability number. The outage then shows up only in user complaints and, weeks later, in sampled task-success scores. Measuring degraded share with its own target, 5% in the chapter, makes "we are hiding an incident behind degradation" an explicit SLO violation.

**K2.** Up to 3⁴ = 81 calls, since each layer multiplies the one below. Two mechanisms bound it. *Retrying at one layer* bounds the multiplication per request: only the layer closest to the failure retries, so one request makes at most `max_attempts` calls per dependency. In the chapter that layer is the gateway for model calls and `call_with_retry` in the adapter for other dependencies. A *retry budget* bounds the aggregate: retries may be at most a fixed fraction of recent first attempts, about 10% with `ratio=0.1`, across every request and layer that shares it. That keeps total retry traffic during an outage at a small surcharge, even when some layer still retries.

**K3.** `InvalidRequestError` means our request was wrong. Counting it lets one buggy caller, for example a new prompt that exceeds the context limit, open the circuit and take the provider away from every other feature. `MalformedResponseError` is model behavior with a working transport. Counting it means a prompt regression that produces invalid JSON looks like a provider outage, opens the circuit, and pushes all traffic to the backup and degraded plans, while repair and fallback were the right tools. `DeadlineExceeded` raised by our own budget check means our request ran out of time, possibly because of an upstream stage. Counting it opens the circuit of a healthy dependency whenever retrieval is slow and leaves the model step too little time.

**K4.** Absolute timestamps require synchronized clocks, and two hosts can disagree by hundreds of milliseconds or more. A deadline of "10:00:03.200" sent to a host whose clock runs half a second fast arrives already half spent. Remaining milliseconds are measured on the sender's monotonic clock and re-anchored on the receiver's monotonic clock, so they lose only the network transit time. The receiver caps the value with its own ceiling because the header is input from another service. A buggy or malicious caller sending a huge budget must not make a service hold resources for an hour, and every service should enforce its own maximum work per request.

**K5.** A `nack` is an explicit report from a live worker that the attempt failed. It carries the error, and the queue schedules the retry with backoff or dead-letters the job. A lease expiry is the absence of any report: the worker died, hung, or lost its connection, and the queue reclaims the job when it next looks. Expired leases must count as attempts because the most dangerous jobs never produce a `nack`. A document that crashes the parser kills its worker each time. If the expiry does not count, the job cycles forever, taking down a worker on each delivery and never reaching the dead-letter set. Counting deliveries rather than reported failures guarantees termination.

**K6.** Little's law says in-flight work = throughput × time in system. Users experience waiting time, which is queue length divided by drain rate, where drain rate = capacity ÷ service time. Accelerator utilization measures how busy the device is, not how long requests wait. A GPU at 95% utilization with continuous batching can have no queue, or a queue of 200. A provider-backed service shows no local utilization at all while requests queue in front of the gateway. Estimated wait comes straight from queue length and service time, and it compares directly with each request's remaining deadline, which is the quantity admission needs: will this request finish in time if admitted? Utilization also flattens near 100% while wait keeps exploding past the knee, so it gives the least information exactly when it matters.

## Engineering questions

**E1.** The sequence, with the primitive responsible for each step:
1. The `llm:primary` breaker inside the gateway opens within seconds of the failure rate crossing its threshold. The gateway then sends every model call straight to the backup client, because the open-circuit hint does not fit the deadline.
2. The degrade policy sees an open model circuit and forces at least `REDUCED` for new requests: shorter outputs, fewer retrieved chunks, no rerank. This roughly halves tokens per request, which matches the backup's half quota.
3. Admission control sets the operator floor `min_degrade_level=1` automatically from breaker state, or a runbook does it. The batch ceiling drops so that all batch and evaluation work is deferred, which frees the backup's quota for interactive users.
4. The gateway's outbound `RateLimiter` for the backup client is configured to the backup's quota, so the system queues locally instead of collecting 429s. Admission control sees the resulting wait rise and starts rejecting interactive requests that would miss their deadline before the backup is saturated.
5. A breaker on `llm:backup` protects against the cascade. If the backup's 429 rate trips it, the policy goes `STATIC` and ticket creation keeps working.
6. Probes heal the primary. When it closes, the plan returns to normal and deferred batch work resumes.
The test for this design is P4.

**E2.** A starting allocation. Interactive gets 75% of the concurrency, 180 slots over 6 API replicas, which gives admission capacity 30 per replica with an interactive bulkhead of 30. Workers get 60 slots over 3 pods, 20 per pod, enforced as a `batch` bulkhead of 20 with batch priority at admission. Tokens follow the same split. Each API replica's gateway `RateLimiter` gets 1.5M ÷ 6 = 250k tokens per minute, and each worker gets 500k ÷ 3 ≈ 166k. Use a burst well below the per-minute value so a cold start cannot spend a minute's quota in a second. Scaling the API to 12 replicas with unchanged per-replica settings would allow 360 + 60 = 420 concurrent calls and 3.5M tokens per minute against limits of 240 and 2M, and the provider would answer with 429s at exactly the moment traffic peaks. Per-replica limits must be derived from the shared budget divided by the current replica count: inject the replica count at startup, use a small central quota service, or cap autoscaling at the count the budget supports. At 12 replicas, that means 15 slots and 125k tokens per minute each.

**E3.** The tool failure becomes an observation returned to the model, for example "query_metrics failed: metrics backend unavailable (circuit open); do not retry this tool in this run". The model can then answer from `search_tickets` and `get_service_status`, or say what is missing. The tool adapter retries once at most, inside its own breaker and the shared budget. Once the `tool:metrics` circuit is open, calls fail fast without touching the backend. Four limits are hard and enforced by the runtime, not the model: a step budget, the request deadline, a maximum number of consecutive tool failures (for example three), and a ban on repeating the same tool with the same arguments after a failure. When a limit is hit, the run ends with a partial result. That result contains the findings so far with evidence, the tools that failed and why, a `PARTIAL` status with per-step records, and a suggestion to re-run once metrics are available. A run with no usable findings ends `FAILED` with the same record, so a human can pick it up.

**E4.** The queue and worker give at-least-once delivery. With `once(store, f"{job.id}:send_reply", send)` the worker skips the send on any redelivery that happens after the effect was recorded. One gap remains: a crash after the provider accepted the email and before `store.put` ran. The redelivered job sees no record and sends again. Local code cannot close this, because the send and the record are writes to two different systems. Closing it requires the email provider to accept an idempotency key, for example `Idempotency-Key: <job.id>:send_reply`, and deduplicate within a window that is longer than the maximum redelivery delay. The alternative is to make sending a two-phase operation the provider supports: create a draft by key, then send by draft id. The honest product statement is "exactly one email, provided the provider honors our idempotency key", and that requirement belongs in vendor selection.

## Practical exercises

**P1.** Expected implementation: give each time bucket a fixed histogram, for example 16 exponential latency bins from 10 ms to about 330 s. Record the bin in `record_success` and `record_failure` using the duration the wrapper already measures. In `snapshot()`, sum the bins of buckets inside the window and walk the cumulative counts to the 50th and 95th percentile bins, then report each bin's upper edge or a linear interpolation inside it. Store no samples, so memory is fixed per breaker. Acceptance criteria: a test drives 100 calls with known durations via `ManualClock.advance` inside the wrapped function and asserts p50 and p95 within one bin of the truth. A second test advances past the window and asserts the percentiles reset. A concurrency test from several threads checks the counts. `slow_call_s` behavior is unchanged.

**P2.** Expected implementation: `AsyncWorker` holds an `asyncio.Semaphore(n)` and a set of tasks. A loop leases jobs while slots are free and spawns a task per job. Each task runs the handler with a context whose `checkpoint` is the same as the sync worker's, plus a background heartbeat task that calls `queue.extend` every `visibility_timeout_s / 3` and cancels the handler with `LeaseLost` if an extend fails. On shutdown the worker stops leasing, sets the stop event, and awaits all tasks with a drain timeout. Handlers that checkpoint release their jobs. Handlers still running at the timeout are cancelled without ack, so their leases expire and the jobs are redelivered. Synchronous queue calls run through `asyncio.to_thread` for the Redis adapter. Acceptance criteria: the queue contract suite still passes, and peak concurrency never exceeds n with 50 jobs. Shutdown during 10 running jobs ends with every job either succeeded or released with attempts unchanged. A handler that runs for three visibility timeouts with heartbeats is never redelivered, and one whose heartbeat fails stops at its next checkpoint.

**P3.** Expected implementation: add `kv_budget_tokens` to `AdmissionConfig`. The cost of a request is `prompt_tokens + max_output_tokens`, multiplied by bytes per token in KV if you want memory units (Chapter 34 gives the formula). The controller tracks reserved tokens instead of request count. Admission requires `reserved + cost <= budget`, the waiting room is measured in tokens, and estimated wait uses reserved tokens drained at the observed token throughput. `release` frees the reservation. Acceptance criteria: with a budget of 64k tokens, one request with a 60k prompt and a 4k output budget is admitted and a second request of any size is rejected or queued. Sixteen requests of 4k each fill the budget equally, and a seventeenth is refused. A test asserts that both scenarios leave the same `utilization()`. Existing count-based tests pass when `kv_budget_tokens` is unset.

**P4.** Expected implementation: give the backup a `ChaosLLM` whose fault depends on load rather than time. A small wrapper counts in-flight calls, or calls per simulated second on the `ManualClock`, and raises `RateLimitError` above a threshold. The primary gets a permanent `outage`. Drive 100 requests per simulated second through `triage_ticket` for 60 seconds. On code where the degrade policy does not react to an open model circuit, assert that the test fails: backup 429s exceed 20% and the `llm:backup` breaker opens. On the chapter's code it should pass. Plans move to `REDUCED` once `llm:primary` opens, and admission defers batch traffic. Assert that the backup 429 rate stays under the threshold, that the `llm:backup` breaker stays closed, and that at least 95% of results are `COMPLETE` or `PARTIAL`. Without admission it may also be necessary to reduce `max_tokens` in the plan so that the token-based limit on the backup is respected. Note this in the test docstring.

## Debugging exercises

**D1.** The minimum-volume condition can never be met on a single replica. Each replica sees about 3 requests per second, so a 10-second window holds about 30 outcomes. That holds if the breaker wraps the whole gateway call, as here, rather than each client inside it. With `min_calls=50` the breaker never evaluates the failure rate, however high it is, and spreading load over more replicas makes it worse. The fix is to put a breaker per client inside the gateway, where each attempt is an outcome. Also size `min_calls` below the volume a replica actually sees in a window, for example 10, or lengthen the window. The fourfold outbound rate is retry amplification. At 60% failure, one layer with three attempts makes 1 + 0.6 + 0.36 ≈ 1.96 calls per request, and two nested layers make about 1.96² ≈ 3.8, which is the observed 4×. Something above the gateway, the chain or the client, is retrying too, and there is no retry budget. Telemetry that confirms it: spans with `attempt > 1` under parent spans that are themselves retries, breaker `calls` per window below `min_calls` in `snapshot()`, and the ratio of provider calls to API requests.

**D2.** The visibility timeout of 60 seconds is now shorter than the handler's run time of 70 to 90 seconds, and the handler does not heartbeat. Every lease expires mid-job. The queue reclaims the job and another worker starts it while the first is still running, so duplicate work happens, and the first worker's ack is refused. Each expiry counts as an attempt, so after `max_attempts` deliveries the job is dead-lettered with "lease expired on final attempt", although no attempt actually failed. The longer termination grace period is a red herring: it affects shutdown, not leases. Telemetry: `lease_lost` outcomes rising right after the deploy, several `job.process` spans per job id with overlapping time ranges, and handler duration p95 above the visibility timeout. Fix: set the visibility timeout well above p99 handler time, call `ctx.heartbeat()` at least every third of the timeout during the embedding step, and alert when handler p95 exceeds half the visibility timeout.

**D3.** The missing protection is isolation between workloads: there is no bulkhead and no batch priority for evaluation runs. The evaluation runner calls the gateway directly and bypasses the admission controller. That is why admission shows low utilization and no rejections while the gateway's shared concurrency semaphore and the provider's rate limit are saturated by evaluation calls, and interactive requests queue behind them. Telemetry that confirms it: the gateway span attribute `queue_ms` for interactive requests rising at 02:00 and 14:00, rate-limiter waits or provider 429s at those times, and in-flight gateway calls broken down by caller showing the evaluation service holding most slots. Fix: send evaluation traffic through admission with batch priority, so it is deferred whenever interactive utilization passes the ceiling. Give it its own `eval` bulkhead sized to a small share of the provider budget, or a separate gateway instance with its own rate limiter and quota.
