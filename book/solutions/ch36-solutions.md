# Chapter 36 — Solutions

Identifiers match the exercises in `book/chapters/36-system-design-cases-2.md`. Numbers are illustrative and reuse the chapter's figures.

## Knowledge questions

**K1.**

| Case | Architecture class | Dominant step | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Research agent | Bounded agent | 4, retrieval (with 8, evaluation, close behind) | The product is the evidence set; budgets, credibility, dedup, and the sufficiency check are all retrieval design, and research quality cannot be judged without gold facts |
| Analytics assistant | LLM-enhanced application with one guarded tool | 7, security (step 5 is the implementation of it) | The model's output is executed; the guard, role, plan check, and blocked columns are where correctness and safety are enforced |
| Workflow automation | LLM-enhanced deterministic workflow | 2, architecture, together with 5, tools | The whole value is in the state machine, pause states, idempotent side effects, and audit trail; the model calls are small |
| Serving platform | Platform | 9, scaling (capacity model) with 6, memory as physical KV | Admission by KV budget and the operating point before the queueing knee define the platform |
| Evaluation platform | Platform | 2, architecture as a data model | Versioned, immutable cases, runs, metrics, and judges are what make comparisons meaningful; everything else is plumbing |

**K2.** Synthesis inside the loop makes the planner commit to conclusions early; later iterations search for confirmation rather than coverage, counter-evidence is under-fetched, and the synthesizer's partial drafts consume context that should hold evidence summaries. It also couples two prompts with different jobs, so a change to one regresses the other. Removing the verifier and asking the model to "cite carefully" leaves citation support unmeasured and unenforced: the synthesizer will attach plausible evidence IDs to claims the evidence does not entail, and nothing downstream notices. The verifier is what turns "cite carefully" into a measured citation support rate and a rewrite loop for unsupported claims.

**K3.** Both are necessary because they fail differently. The guard catches intent-level problems the database cannot see: a query with no `LIMIT` (the role allows it; the guard adds one), a `pg_sleep` call (the role may permit the function), a query against an allowlisted table that selects a blocked column through an alias the role's grants happen to permit, and it produces a message the assistant can act on ("table hr.salaries is not on the allowlist"). The role catches what the guard misses because of parser gaps or bugs: a dialect construct the parser does not recognize that reaches a forbidden table, a function the forbidden list did not anticipate, cross-tenant rows (row-level security is a database concept the guard cannot evaluate), and a bypass where someone calls the executor without the guard. Column-level grants also stop deliberate aggregation over personal-data columns regardless of how the SQL is written.

**K4.** Request count ignores sequence length, and KV memory is proportional to tokens. Ten 32,000-token requests hold the KV that a hundred 3,300-token requests would, so a count-based admission policy admits a few long requests and then starves everything else while utilization looks moderate. With the chapter's figures (0.33 MB per token): one hundred interactive requests of about 3,300 tokens each need 100 × 3,300 × 0.33 MB ≈ 109 GB; one 32,000-token request needs about 10.6 GB; so roughly ten long requests equal one hundred typical ones.

**K5.** The judge is part of the measuring instrument, not the thing measured. If a judge prompt or model changes and scores shift, the shift says nothing about the system under test. Recording the judge version on every result lets the comparison tool refuse to compare runs scored by different judges, or rescore the baseline with the new judge first. Without this, a dashboard shows a step change in groundedness or quality on the day the judge changed, teams open regression investigations against their own code, and over time nobody trusts the trend line because it is unclear which steps were real.

## Engineering questions

**E1.** Decide with the evaluation set, not by intuition. Add 20 gold questions that analysts consider "deep" (more sub-questions, more conflicting sources) and measure, at the current budgets, coverage and budget-exhaustion rate on that slice. If exhaustion is high and coverage low on deep questions while shallow questions finish early, the budgets are binding and worth raising for the deep slice only. Raise one budget at a time: iterations first (cheapest, often the binding constraint because sufficiency is not reached), then documents, then searches. Watch coverage, citation support rate, source quality mix, iterations to sufficiency, cost per report, and p95 wall time. Expect cost to rise roughly linearly with documents extracted (the extractor dominates the token table) and citation support rate to stay flat or fall slightly, because more evidence means more claims and more chances for weak support. Implement "depth" as a per-request budget tier rather than a global change, and keep the hard cost ceiling.

**E2.** Instrument the long-tail path: log each generated SQL with the question, the tables and aggregations used, and whether the answer was accepted or edited. Weekly, cluster long-tail questions by normalized intent (metric-like expression, dimensions, filters) and rank clusters by volume and by acceptance rate. The top clusters are candidate metrics; a draft definition is generated from the most-accepted SQL in the cluster and proposed, never auto-published. Ownership: each metric has a business owner (the team whose report defines it, usually finance or operations) who approves the definition and a technical owner (an analyst) who maintains the SQL expression and tests. Add a "promote to metric" button in the analyst view so analysts can nominate a long-tail answer. Track semantic-layer share, long-tail acceptance rate, and the number of metrics with stale owners. Promoting the top 15 clusters typically moves the share by 10 to 15 points; the last few points come from synonyms added to existing metrics rather than new ones.

**E3.** The credit check belongs after policy checks and before approval, because approvers should see its result, and it should only run for cases that passed the cheaper checks (sanctions, duplicates), to avoid paying the fee for cases that will be rejected anyway. It is a side-effecting, billable call, so: an idempotency key derived from workflow ID plus step plus the normalized vendor identifiers (tax ID), a check-before-call against the step's own store so a retried step does not pay twice, bounded retries only on transport errors and never on a bureau "no record" response, and a cached result with a validity window (for example 30 days per tax ID) so that a resubmitted case reuses it. The audit record contains the request identifiers sent, the bureau's reference number, the returned score or band, the policy threshold applied, the timestamp, the fee, and the decision derived. If the bureau is down for a day: the step's retries exhaust, the case moves to a "waiting on external" sub-state with an SLA timer rather than the exception queue, a scheduled retry runs hourly, the dashboard shows the count of waiting cases, and after the SLA threshold the controller can approve a documented manual override that is itself an audited event.

**E4.** A 12,000-token prefix on every request from that team raises its per-request KV footprint from about 1.1 GB to about 5 GB (15,300 tokens × 0.33 MB). If the team contributes a fifth of interactive traffic, average resident KV per request rises from about 2.0 GB (0.9 × 1.1 GB + 0.1 × 10.6 GB, the chapter's mix) to about 2.8 GB, roughly 40 percent, resident sequences per replica fall accordingly, and the pool crosses its queueing knee at a lower offered load; TTFT p95 rises for everyone, not only that team, and prefill compute for the 12,000 tokens adds directly to TTFT for that team. Alternatives: first, prefix caching for the shared system prompt, which removes the repeated prefill and, in engines that share the cached prefix's KV blocks across requests, most of the memory cost; measure the hit rate, since it only helps if the prefix is byte-identical. Second, shorten the prompt through context engineering (Chapter 5): move static reference material into retrieval so only relevant parts are included, and target a 3,000-token system prompt. A third option is to route that team to its own replica with a token-weighted quota, which protects the others but does not fix the cost.

## Practical exercises

**P1.** Expected implementation: add `max_joins: int | None` to `GuardConfig`; in the sqlglot engine count `exp.Join` nodes and detect joins without an `on`/`using` clause (cross joins) as a violation `cross_join`; in the regex engine count `\bjoin\b` occurrences and flag `cross join` or a `join` not followed by `on`/`using` before the next clause keyword. Add an optional `explain: Callable[[str], dict] | None` argument to `SqlGuard.check` (or to the constructor) and a `max_estimated_rows` config; when provided, call it with the approved SQL, read the estimated rows, and reject with `plan_too_expensive` if over the cap; when not provided, record `plan_check_skipped` in a `notes` field without failing. Acceptance: parametrized tests over both engines; `SELECT * FROM fact_orders, dim_customer` rejected as `cross_join`; a two-table inner join with an `ON` clause passes; a fake `explain` returning a large estimate causes rejection and a small one passes; existing tests still pass.

**P2.** Expected implementation: normalize column names (lowercase, strip quotes and whitespace, collapse underscores), align columns by name, convert rows to tuples in the aligned order, compare as multisets (`collections.Counter`) with a custom numeric comparison using relative tolerance plus a small absolute tolerance for values near zero, treat `None` and `NaN` as equal to themselves only, and compare strings after trimming. Provide `explain_difference(a, b) -> str | None` that reports the first mismatch (missing column, row count, or first differing row). Acceptance: tests for row order, column order, case and underscore differences in column names, floats differing at 1e-7 passing and at 1e-3 failing, an extra row failing, and the explanation naming the right cause.

**P3.** Expected implementation: states and transitions as in the chapter's diagram, built on the Chapter 17 engine's typed state, retry policy, and pause/resume; a `FakeERP` with `create_vendor(external_ref, payload)` that raises a transient error on the first call 30 percent of the time (seeded) and stores vendors by external reference, plus `find_by_external_ref`; a `FakeApprover` that can be configured to approve, reject, or never respond; an append-only audit list with entries `(workflow_id, step, actor, event, timestamp, payload_hash)`. The create step must call `find_by_external_ref` before `create_vendor`. Acceptance: run 200 seeded workflows and assert the fake ERP holds exactly one vendor per approved workflow; assert every state transition has an audit entry whose actor is `system`, `model`, or a named approver; configure the approver to never respond and assert the case reaches the exception state when the timer fires; raise a fatal error in the ERP step, assert the exception state, resolve, and assert resumption from the create step without re-running extraction.

**P4.** Expected implementation: pydantic models `Case` (id, version, input, fixtures, expected_facts, rubric, tags, owner, acl), `Suite` (id, version, case_refs as `(case_id, version)` tuples), `Run` (id, suite_ref, app_version, model_versions dict, prompt_versions dict, index_version, tool_versions dict, seed, evaluator_versions dict, started_at, finished_at, cost_usd), `Result` (run_id, case_id, output, trace_ref, metrics dict, trials list), `MetricDef` (name, version, kind: deterministic or judge, judge_version). An `InMemoryStore` with `freeze_suite(suite_id)` called implicitly when a run references it, after which `update_case` on a case version in that suite raises `ImmutableError`. A `compare(run_a, run_b)` function returning per-case deltas, per-slice aggregates by tag, and a `warnings` list containing `judge_version_changed:<metric>` when evaluator versions differ. Acceptance: tests for the comparison output on a small fixture, the warning on judge change, and the immutability error.

## Debugging exercises

**D1.** Hypotheses, in checking order. First, the web search tool's results changed (ranking or an aggregator now dominating), so the planner needs more iterations to find primary sources: check `search.result_domains` and the credibility tier distribution per report over time; a shift toward low-tier domains confirms it. Second, the dedup stage is failing (a canonicalization bug or an embedding model change moved the near-duplicate threshold), so the two-source sufficiency rule is never satisfied: check `dedup.removed_count` per report and the near-duplicate score distribution; a drop in removals with a rise in `evidence.sources_per_subquestion` that are near-identical confirms it. Third, the planner or extractor model version changed under the alias (provider-side), producing more sub-questions or weaker extraction: check `model.version` in planner spans and the sub-question count per report. Coverage staying flat while exhaustion rises points at the loop not converging rather than the questions being harder, which is why the first two hypotheses rank above the third.

**D2.** Look first at the data-freshness and definition dimensions, not at the SQL. The most likely single trace attribute is the answer cache's `freshness_marker` (last load timestamp of the tables involved) compared with the current load timestamp: a 4 percent gap on "last quarter" is the signature of an answer cached before a late-arriving load (returns or adjustments posted after quarter end). If the marker matches, the next candidate is the metric definition version: the finance report may use a `net_revenue` definition revised this quarter while the semantic layer compiled the previous version (`metric.version` in the trace). Self-consistency agreeing 3 of 3 and the gold set passing are both consistent with either cause: the candidates share the stale cache or the same definition, and the gold set is frozen on a snapshot.

**D3.** Pull KV utilization and queue wait time per pool first, then admission rejections per team. Utilization at 55 percent with a normal request rate and doubled TTFT is the signature of KV-bound saturation: resident sequences are full of long contexts, so new requests wait in the admission queue while compute sits idle. The most likely cause given the chapter's design is that the upgrade changed the admission policy or the pool configuration so that long-context (32,000-token) requests are no longer capped or are being admitted to the interactive pool by count rather than token budget, or a team's traffic mix shifted to long RAG contexts after the upgrade. Confirm by plotting admitted tokens per request over the window and the share of requests above 16,000 tokens; the fix is restoring token-budget admission and a per-pool context cap.
